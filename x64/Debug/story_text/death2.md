Morning hits like a slap.

Alarm, cold air, empty apartment.
No sounds from Mom's room, she's already gone to work, or never really came back from the night shift. Kitchen note, quick bite of something that barely counts as breakfast, jacket, backpack, door.

The city outside is the same as always: too early, too grey, too busy for someone who doesn't really want to be anywhere.

I take the usual route. City bus to the edge of town, then the special **lucky losers** school bus that drags us out to the other settlement. By now my legs walk it on autopilot. My brain doesn't have to help.

The school bus waits where it always does, coughing exhaust into the cold air.
I tap my card, the reader beeps, and I climb in.

Inside it's almost empty.
It usually is.

Dim lights, worn-out seats, a couple of old people in the front, bags on their knees. That's it. The back rows are mine. They always are.

I don't even look at others. I just head straight there, like I do every morning.

Step, step, aisle, last row.
Left side, by the window.
My spot.

I drop into the seat

and freeze.

Something is wrong.

It's that tiny, stupid feeling you get when you walk into your own room and a picture on the wall is crooked, or a chair is slightly moved. You can't name it immediately, but every nerve says: this is not how it's supposed to be.

I feel eyes on the side of my face.

I turn my head.

She's sitting there.

Right next to me, in the corner seat by the window. I didn't see her when I got on, the seat in front must've blocked her completely. Now she's suddenly there, like the bus decided to grow a passenger out of thin air.

A girl with a languid look, strangely familiar and yet so pleasant.

She's leaning against the glass, shoulder almost touching the foggy window, chin resting lightly on her hand. Her hair falls forward, hiding part of her face, but I can see the curve of her cheek, the line of her nose, the faint dark half-moons under her eyes. Not the **I stayed up gaming** kind of tired, more like the **life has been chewing on me for a while** kind.

Big over-ear headphones cover her ears.
A cable runs down into the pocket of her coat.
The world on this bus barely seems to exist for her.

For a second everything feels... off-balance.
Like I've seen this before.
Not her, exactly, but this frame: girl by the window, grey morning behind the glass, that way of staring outside as if she's trying to remember something that happened far away.

The engine rumbles, the doors hiss shut. The bus starts moving.

At first I don't really notice the sound leaking from her headphones. It's just a faint beat under the noise of the motor and the squeak of old plastic. But then it nudges the back of my mind, like a finger poking at a bruise.

I know that rhythm.

I focus without turning my head too much.
The melody rises, dips, hits a pause and comes back in exactly where my hands expect it to. There's a small stutter before the main beat returns a glitch I know very well, a detail I actually liked.

My stomach drops.

This is the track.
From my game.

Maybe not the exact same file the mix feels a bit cleaner, the highs softer but the skeleton is identical. Same progression, same timing, same little **mistakes**.

She's listening to it like it's just another song in her playlist.
Like it's not a piece of a world I only ever expected to find inside my screen.

I stare at the seat in front of me, fingers curling around the torn fabric.

I could say something.
Ask where she got it. Ask if she plays. Ask... anything.

My throat doesn't agree.

Words line up, then fall apart before they reach my tongue.
What if I'm wrong?
What if it's some similar track and I just sound insane?

So I stay quiet.

The bus keeps rolling.
Fields slide by, then low houses, then the sad little main street of the settlement.
She keeps looking out the window, lost somewhere far away, music only she can really hear wrapping around her like a second layer of air.


Hours passed...
When we finally stop in front of the school, she stands up first.

For a heartbeat I expect her to glance at me.
She doesn't.

She pulls her backpack on, walks down the aisle, steps out into the cold morning
and heads toward the same building I'm supposed to walk into.

I follow a few steps behind, keeping just enough distance to pretend it's a coincidence.

Maybe it is.

Maybe it's nothing.

But as the school doors swallow us both, the only thing I can really hear is that track still echoing in my head from her headphones and from the game that was never supposed to leave my screen.

I decided to meet her on the way home, because the bus only goes back once a day after classes are over.

With that thought, I made my way to class.
